\begin{abstract}
	Understanding animal vocalizations means connecting acoustic form to communicative meaning. Years of field studies have produced extensive knowledge of such mappings, yet no unified framework exists for comparing them across species. We introduce \dbname, a cross-species repertoire database comprising \ncalls calls from \nspecies species spanning mammals, birds, and amphibians, annotated with standardized semantic and acoustic descriptions synthesized from the scientific literature. Using \dbname, we characterize what animals communicate about and how. Acoustic and semantic distances are significantly correlated within species, across related species, and across families, providing evidence for form-to-meaning structure that extends across phylogenetic boundaries. We leverage this structure to identify cross-species acoustic--semantic motifs and to support lexical exploration between repertoires. These results suggest that a biological code linking acoustic form to communicative function may be a broad organizational principle of animal communication, and that large-scale repertoire synthesis---now made practical by language models---unlocks new possibilities for comparative bioacoustics. An interactive tool provides the complete results and supports exploration at scale.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Decades of field research have established that animal vocalizations are not simple reflexes but structured communicative signals whose forms are shaped by function \cite{marler1955characteristics}. Alarm calls are short, broadband, and easily localized---suited for rapid threat broadcast. Contact calls are tonal and repetitive---suited for long-distance propagation and individual recognition. Infant-directed calls are high-pitched across taxa---plausibly linked to shared attentional mechanisms. Yet these patterns have been documented one species at a time, and no synthesis has asked whether they constitute a universal code: a systematic, learnable relationship between acoustic form and communicative meaning that holds across the tree of life.

The question is not merely empirical but conceptual. In linguistics, the arbitrariness of the sign---the principle that a word's sound bears no necessary relation to its meaning---has been a foundational tenet since Saussure \cite{saussure1916}. Counter-evidence is accumulating: sound symbolism and crosslinguistic iconicity demonstrate that form-to-meaning mappings exist and are learned in human language \cite{dingemanse2015arbitrariness}. In non-human animals the question is more open still. If such a biological code exists, it could arise through two mechanisms: convergent evolution driven by shared physical and ecological constraints (e.g., the localizability demands of alarm signaling), or phylogenetic conservation of ancestral form-to-meaning associations within lineages. These mechanisms make different predictions---the former produces cross-taxon universals, the latter family-level regularities---and can in principle be distinguished with sufficient data.

Here we test for a biological code at scale by assembling \dbname, a database of \ncalls calls from \nspecies species (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}). Each entry pairs a free-text acoustic description with a free-text semantic description, standardized ontology keywords, confidence, and references synthesized from literature search. We embed both descriptions independently into continuous vector spaces and ask whether their pairwise distance structures are aligned---within species, across species, and across higher taxa. A simple linear model trained on this alignment is evaluated on held-out calls, species, and families. We use the resulting spaces to identify cross-species acoustic--semantic motifs and provide an interactive web application for broad exploration.

\dbname was assembled by prompting a large language model to structure knowledge from peer-reviewed literature for each target species, producing entries that record call name, free-text acoustic description, free-text semantic description, standardized ontology keywords, confidence, and references (see Methods). The \nspecies species span three vertebrate classes: \nmammals mammals, \nbirds birds, and \namphibians amphibians, covering taxa from African savanna elephants (\textit{Loxodonta africana}) and gray wolves (\textit{Canis lupus}) to Carolina chickadees (\textit{Poecile carolinensis}) and t\'{u}ngara frogs (\textit{Engystomops pustulosus}).

The most common semantic keywords are attention ($n = \nattention$), contact ($n = \ncontact$), group coordination ($n = \ngroupcoordination$), affiliation ($n = \naffiliation$), and alarm ($n = \nalarm$; Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}A). The most frequent acoustic features are short ($n = \nshort$), repetitive ($n = \nrepetitive$), and tonal ($n = \ntonal$; Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset}B). Together these annotation axes provide the substrate for testing form-to-meaning alignment.

We embedded each call's acoustic and semantic descriptions separately into a shared sentence-transformer space (see Methods) and computed pairwise cosine similarities across all \ncallpairs call pairs. Blocked Mantel tests \cite{mantel1967detection} show positive acoustic--semantic alignment within species ($r = \rWithinSpecies$, $p \pWithinSpecies$, $n = \nWithinSpeciesPairs$ pairs), among different species in the same family ($r = \rSameFamily$, $p \pSameFamily$, $n = \nSameFamilyPairs$), and among species in different families ($r = \rDifferentFamilies$, $p \pDifferentFamilies$, $n = \nDifferentFamiliesPairs$; Fig.~\ref{fig:embeddings}).

The same-family coefficient is the strongest of the three, although the difference is modest. This pattern is compatible with a contribution from shared evolutionary history, while the positive different-family coefficient shows that the association is not restricted to close relatives. We trained models to predict semantic embeddings from acoustic embeddings under three hold-out conditions (Fig.~\ref{fig:prediction}). For held-out calls, ridge regression achieves cosine similarity \RidgeHeldOutCallsCosine and MRR \RidgeHeldOutCallsMRR, compared with \RandomHeldOutCallsCosine and \RandomHeldOutCallsMRR for random pairing. Acoustic nearest-neighbour retrieval yields lower cosine similarity but higher MRR in every split, indicating that prediction and retrieval emphasize different aspects of the mapping.

AnimalLex allows best guess translations across the animal kingdom. Given a call from species~A, we retrieve the call in species~B whose semantic embedding is nearest---a lexical translation from one repertoire to another. When both species are represented in \dbname with full semantic annotations, the mapping is performed in semantic space by retrieval. Given the form-to-meaning structure identified above, translational attempts are also possible on the basis of acoustic annotations only---as will routinely be the case for poorly studied taxa---the acoustic-to-semantic predictor from the previous section supplies a best-guess semantic embedding from acoustic features alone, enabling translation even across the boundary of documented knowledge.

This two-mode pipeline (retrieval for annotated calls; prediction for unannotated ones) makes \dbname a living resource: every new species added expands the reach of translation, and the predictor provides a scaffold for annotating the next. The web application (\url{https://emmanuel-dev.tail3ea00b.ts.net/}) exposes the complete motif results and allows users to inspect correspondences interactively (see Appendix~\ref{sec:supp_webapp}). At the default acoustic and semantic thresholds of \motifacousticthreshold and \motifsemanticthreshold, respectively, the include-only analysis returns \nmotifs motifs. Figure~\ref{fig:landmarks} is intentionally reserved for a small manually selected subset whose provenance will remain the web application's full result set.

A natural extension is to learn the translation \emph{directly in acoustic space}, bypassing the semantic intermediate. Two species that are phylogenetically close should have nearly aligned acoustic spaces---similar vocal anatomies, shared evolutionary pressures, conserved call repertoires---and their repertoires should be mappable by a small rotation. Phylogenetically distant species may require a larger transformation. This suggests learning a family of species-pair rotations $R_{AB}$ (Procrustes-style, constrained to be orthogonal) regularised by phylogenetic distance: $\|R_{AB} - \mathbf{I}\|_F \leq \lambda \cdot d_{\mathrm{phylo}}(A,B)$, where $d_{\mathrm{phylo}}$ is branch length in a dated phylogeny and $\lambda$ controls how much divergence is allowed per unit evolutionary distance. Such a model would be fully audio-grounded, require no semantic labels, and could scale to the thousands of species for which recordings but no annotations exist. We leave this to future work, but note that the form-to-meaning results above provide the theoretical justification: because acoustic and semantic spaces are aligned, a rotation in acoustic space is also, approximately, a rotation in semantic space.

\section{Discussion}

Our results establish three things. First, animal vocalizations carry systematic form-to-meaning relationships detectable across the tree of life---not just within well-studied model systems. Second, these relationships are learnable and generalizable: a model trained on some species predicts communicative meaning from acoustics for entirely held-out species and families. Third, this learnability enables practical cross-species lexical translation---finding, for any call, its closest semantic analogue in any other repertoire---including for species whose calls have never been semantically annotated.

Two non-exclusive mechanisms can explain the cross-taxon alignment. Convergent functional pressures may independently channel evolution toward similar acoustic solutions for similar communicative problems: the physics of sound localization favors broadband calls for alarm regardless of ancestry \cite{marler1955characteristics}; the acoustics of long-range propagation favor tonality for cohesion calls \cite{morton1977on}. Phylogenetic inertia may additionally preserve ancestral form-to-meaning associations within lineages, consistent with the somewhat stronger same-family coefficient. These mechanisms predict different patterns in phylogenetically controlled analyses, which future work with broader taxonomic sampling can test.

Several caveats apply. \dbname represents the current state of synthesized knowledge, which skews toward well-studied mammals; birds and amphibians---acoustically diverse and experimentally tractable---are underrepresented. Our acoustic embeddings are derived from textual descriptions, not from audio directly; future work integrating audio representations \cite{hagiwara2022avesanimalvocalizationencoder,robinson2025naturelmaudio} will test whether the form-to-meaning alignment is stronger in the raw signal domain. Lexical translation should be understood as semantic proximity within the space of documented ethological descriptions---not as a claim about subjective experience, cognitive content, or the \textit{Umwelt} of the signaling animal. Any mapping through human language inherits an additional anthropomorphic bias and should be treated as a heuristic rather than a literal gloss.

Despite these limitations, the demonstration that form-to-meaning structure is learnable and cross-taxon generalizable opens a practical path forward: with each new species added to \dbname, the translation model improves, and with each improvement the model can bootstrap annotation of the next species. This virtuous cycle---human knowledge, compressed by language models, refined by acoustic-semantic prediction, extended to undocumented taxa---is the long-term promise of the approach introduced here.

\section{Methods}

\paragraph{Database construction}
\dbname was assembled by prompting a large language model with a structured schema specifying six required fields per call: (1) call name, (2) free-text acoustic description (spectro-temporal features, amplitude envelope, call-internal structure), (3) free-text semantic description (communicative function, behavioral context, receiver responses), (4) ontology keywords drawn from a fixed controlled vocabulary, (5) subjective confidence (high, medium, or low), and (6) references. The present manuscript reports the web application's default species-fit view, restricted to species marked \emph{include}, while retaining all call-confidence levels. The application additionally exposes \emph{include + caution} and all-species views, alongside confidence-filtered sensitivity views.

\paragraph{Embeddings}

Acoustic and semantic descriptions were embedded independently using the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 sentence transformer \cite{reimers2019sentence}, which maps variable-length text to a 384-dimensional vector space trained on semantic similarity tasks. Embeddings were $\ell_2$-normalized before computing cosine distances. We deliberately used textual embeddings rather than audio-derived representations to ensure that acoustic and semantic descriptions were embedded in a comparable space; the limitations of this choice are discussed above.

\paragraph{Mantel test}

For each subset of calls (within species, same-family cross-species, and different-family pairs), we computed the Pearson correlation between acoustic and semantic distances. Statistical significance was assessed with the web application's one-sided Mantel test using blocked label permutations: semantic call identities were shuffled within species. The shared bundle used for this manuscript and the app contains \npermutations permutations.

\paragraph{PMI analysis}

Acoustic and semantic keywords were taken from the structured annotation fields. Keywords occurring in fewer than four calls were excluded. For each acoustic--semantic keyword pair $(a, s)$, PMI was estimated as $\log_2 \hat{p}(a,s) - \log_2 \hat{p}(a) - \log_2 \hat{p}(s)$, where probabilities were estimated from call frequencies across the include-only analysis dataset. Significance was assessed by shuffling complete acoustic-keyword sets among all included calls while holding semantic-keyword sets fixed, using \npermutations permutations. We controlled the false-discovery rate across all tested cells using the Benjamini--Hochberg procedure.

\paragraph{Acoustic-to-semantic prediction}

We trained a ridge regression mapping acoustic embeddings $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ to semantic embeddings $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^{384}$ and compared it with random pairing and acoustic nearest-neighbour retrieval. Predicted embeddings were $\ell_2$-normalized before evaluation. Performance was measured by (i) mean cosine similarity between predicted and true semantic embeddings and (ii) mean reciprocal rank (MRR) of the true call's semantic embedding among candidates, ranked by cosine similarity to the prediction. Evaluation used \nfolds outer folds for held-out calls, species, and families; error bars are 95\% percentile bootstrap intervals across outer folds.

\paragraph{Heavy AI methods}

This work made maximally heavy use of AI technology including database collection, curation and organization, discussion, design and implementation of analyses, literature search, writing and reviewing.

\subsection{Human non-linguistic vocal bursts}
\label{sec:supp_human_bursts}

A stronger test of the biological code's universality is to include \emph{human} non-linguistic vocalizations --- short affective bursts such as laughter, gasps, sighs, growls, and cries --- in the same framework as animal calls.
Unlike language-mediated glosses, these vocalizations are pre-lexical, cross-culturally produced and recognized \cite{sauter2010crosscultural}, and share evolutionary continuity with homologous calls in other primates.
We curated a database of 30 human vocal burst types following the 30-category taxonomy of \citet{cowen2019mapping}, who collected and rated 2{,}032 non-speech human vocal burst stimuli.
Each burst type received the same annotation schema as animal calls in \dbname: an acoustic description (phonetic form, pitch contour, voice quality, duration) and a semantic description (affective context and social function), with ontology keywords drawn from the shared \dbname vocabulary (\emph{alarm}, \emph{affiliative}, \emph{display}, \emph{distress}, etc.) augmented by two new valence keywords (\emph{positive\_affect}, \emph{negative\_affect}).

\paragraph{Semantic correspondents.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_correspondents} shows the maximum semantic cosine similarity between each of the 30 human burst types and each animal species in \dbname.
Several interpretable patterns emerge.
Fear and pain bursts --- high-pitched, sudden, broadband exclamations --- consistently map to alarm and anti-predator calls across all three vertebrate classes, with particularly high similarity to bat echolocation-adjacent distress calls and primate alarm barks.
Contentment, serenity, and adoration bursts map most strongly to affiliative calls in social primates and songbirds, consistent with the predicted association between soft, low-frequency vocalizations and affiliation.
The ``Guilt'' row, acoustically similar to a soft descending murmur, is weakest across the board, reflecting the absence of a clear non-human functional analogue.

\paragraph{Keyword-level form-to-meaning structure.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_pmi} applies the same PMI analysis as Fig.~\ref{fig:pmi} to the human burst database.
The acoustic rows are identical; the semantic columns reflect the human keyword vocabulary.
Several patterns echo the biological code identified in non-human animals:
\emph{high-frequency} calls are strongly associated with \emph{alarm} and \emph{referential} functions (fear gasp, surprise burst, pain exclamation; PMI~$\approx$~2.3), paralleling the high-frequency $\times$ alarm enrichment across taxa.
\emph{Broadband/noisy} calls are enriched for \emph{negative affect} (anger, disgust; PMI~$\approx$~1.3), consistent with Morton's frequency code \cite{morton1977on}.
\emph{Repetitive} and \emph{pulsed} bursts are enriched for \emph{display} and \emph{contact} functions (laughter; PMI~$\approx$~1.3), and \emph{long/sustained} calls are depleted for \emph{display} (soft sighs and murmurs are not attention-grabbing; PMI~$\approx -1.7$).

To quantify how much the overall pattern overlaps with the non-human animal PMI (Fig.~\ref{fig:pmi}), we computed the Pearson correlation between the two matrices restricted to their shared keywords (alarm, affiliative, display, contact, distress, referential): $\rho_\mathrm{PMI} = -0.03$.
This \emph{PMI pattern similarity} is a completely different quantity from the call-pair Mantel $r$ reported elsewhere: it asks ``do the same acoustic features get associated with the same semantic categories in both humans and animals?'', not ``does acoustic proximity predict semantic proximity across individual calls?''
A near-zero $\rho_\mathrm{PMI}$ means that the \emph{global rank-ordering} of acoustic--semantic weights differs: individual strong associations are preserved (high-frequency $\to$ alarm, broadband $\to$ negative affect), but their relative magnitudes and the allocation across categories diverge.
The species-level analysis below reveals why: the code runs in opposite directions for mammals and birds, and their contributions cancel in the aggregate.

\paragraph{Human vocalizations in the cross-species Mantel matrix.}
Figure~\ref{fig:human_mantel_row} adds a ``human row'' to the species-pairwise Mantel matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:mantel_matrix}): for each animal species, the Pearson $r$ between acoustic and semantic cosine similarities across all $30 \times n_s$ human--animal call pairs.
Within each class, species are sorted by their $r$ value (highest first), revealing the graded structure of human-animal correspondence.

The two findings above are now reconcilable.
\emph{Mammals} show a consistently positive mean correlation ($\bar{r} = 0.082$), with the strongest alignment in a bat (Egyptian fruit bat, $r = 0.65$), a prosimian (Indri, $r = 0.53$), and several Old World monkeys and apes (Crowned guenon, Chacma baboon, Mandrill; $r \approx 0.38$--$0.41$).
\emph{Amphibians} are weakly positive ($\bar{r} = 0.039$).
\emph{Birds}, strikingly, are on average \emph{negative} ($\bar{r} = -0.126$): for most avian species, the calls that sound most like human bursts tend to carry \emph{opposite} semantic functions.
This inversion --- where the acoustic-semantic code runs in an opposing direction between birds and humans --- is the main driver of the near-zero aggregate PMI correlation.
Averaged across all three classes, their contributions partially cancel, bringing the keyword-level correlation to $r \approx 0$.

The picture that emerges is therefore: at the keyword level, the code appears globally flat because mammalian and avian influences offset each other; breaking by species exposes the underlying structure --- genuine acoustic--semantic sharing with mammals (amplified by evolutionary and ecological proximity), and a largely inverted mapping with birds, whose vocal priorities (territory, mate attraction, species recognition via song) differ fundamentally from human affective burst functions.

\section{Sensitivity analysis}
\label{sec:supp_sensitivity}

Figure~\ref{fig:supp_sensitivity} presents two sensitivity analyses.
Panel~A shows Mantel~$r$ (with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals, $n_\text{boot}=500$) computed separately for within-class and cross-class species pairs.
The form-to-meaning alignment is positive and significant within Mammalia ($r = 0.364$), Aves ($r = 0.377$), and Amphibia ($r = 0.694$), and holds for most cross-class comparisons; the negative value for Aves~$\times$~Amphibia reflects the small sample ($n = 731$ pairs).

Panel~B quantifies how the mutual nearest-neighbour graph changes when the strictness of the cross-modal agreement criterion is relaxed from top-1 to top-$k$ ($k \in \{1,2,3\}$).
At $k=1$ (the main analysis), 4 cliques span all three classes; relaxing to $k=2$ yields 235 such cliques, and $k=3$ yields 10{,}690.
The main-text result is therefore conservative: the qualitative finding strengthens as the criterion is relaxed.
